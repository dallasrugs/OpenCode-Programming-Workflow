# Foundations — Why GTD Works (Welcome + Ch 1)

> Load for almost any GTD question — this is the "why" the whole method rests on.

## The problem GTD solves
Knowledge work has **"no edges."** Allen: *"In the old days work was self-evident… It was clear when the work was finished."* Now almost every project *could* be done better, infinite information is available, inputs explode (hundreds of emails/day), and roles keep shifting. **Traditional time management fails** because calendars, ABC priority codes, and daily to-do lists were built for assembly-line work — *"a calendar… can really effectively manage only a small portion of what you need to organize."* And pure "big-picture / values" thinking fails *alone* too: day-to-day overwhelm blocks you from focusing higher, and clarifying loftier goals just *"raises the bar"* on everything that needs changing.

## The key concepts
- **"Stuff"** (Allen's verbatim definition): *"anything you have allowed into your psychological or physical world that doesn't belong where it is, but for which you haven't yet determined the desired outcome and the next action step."* As long as it's "stuff," it's not controllable — most to-do lists are *"merely listings of 'stuff,' not inventories of the resultant real work… an amorphous blob of undoability."*
- **Open loops / incompletes**: *"anything pulling at your attention that doesn't belong where it is, the way it is"* — from "End world hunger" to "Replace pencil sharpener." Every agreement with yourself, *"big or little, is being tracked by a less-than-conscious part of you."*
- **Mind like water** (the karate image of perfect readiness): throw a pebble in a still pond — it responds *"totally appropriately to the force and mass of the input; then it returns to calm. It doesn't overreact or underreact."* *"Anything that causes you to overreact or underreact can control you, and often does."* This is the "ready state" — the goal.
- **Psychic RAM**: short-term memory works like computer RAM — *"Your conscious mind… is a focusing tool, not a storage place."* The mind has no sense of past/future, so anything stored there it thinks you should be doing *all the time, right now*. *"As soon as you have two things to do stored in your RAM, you've generated personal failure"* — diffuse, sourceless stress.

## The fundamental principle
Most stress comes from *"inappropriately managed commitments."* The fix is to get **100% of your "stuff" out of your head** into a trusted external system you review regularly. *"There is no reason ever to have the same thought twice, unless you like having that thought."* The diagnostic: *"There is usually an inverse proportion between how much something is on your mind and how much it's getting done."* And: *"Things rarely get stuck because of lack of time. They get stuck because the doing of them has not been defined."*

## The two core objectives (verbatim)
> *"(1) capturing all the things that need to get done — now, later, someday, big, little, or in between — into a logical and trusted system outside of your head and off your mind; and (2) disciplining yourself to make front-end decisions about all of the 'inputs' you let into your life so that you will always have a plan for 'next actions' that you can implement or renegotiate at any moment."*

## The three basic requirements for managing commitments
1. **Capture** anything unfinished into a trusted collection bucket outside your mind (that you'll return to regularly).
2. **Clarify** exactly what your commitment is and decide the next move to make progress on it.
3. **Keep reminders** organized in a system you **review** regularly.

## Two orientations
- **Horizontal** — coherence across *all* the things you're engaged in (the "police radar" scan). This is the five-stage workflow (`01`).
- **Vertical** — thinking up and down a *single* project/topic. This is the Natural Planning Model (`04`).

## Work bottom-up
Allen coaches starting with the **mundane runway** (in-basket, current actions), not top-down mission/values — because *"trying to manage from the top down, when the bottom is out of control, may be the least effective approach."* Clearing the low level frees attention for the high level. (See the six horizons, `06`.)

## A note on terminology (2007 vs. modern GTD)
This skill is built from the 2001/2007 edition, which names the five stages **Collect · Process · Organize · Review · Do**. The 2015 revision (and today's GTD community) renames them **Capture · Clarify · Organize · Reflect · Engage** — same steps. This skill uses the modern verbs in conversation and notes the originals where it matters. The mechanics are identical.

## How to apply
For any GTD question, anchor on the principle: **is this "stuff" still in the user's head, or captured into a trusted system with a defined outcome and next action?** Almost every GTD intervention is moving an item from undefined "stuff" → a clarified, organized, reviewable commitment.
