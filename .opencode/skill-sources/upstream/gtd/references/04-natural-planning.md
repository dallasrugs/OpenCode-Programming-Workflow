# Natural Planning — Getting Projects Under Way (Ch 3 + Ch 10)

> Load for: a stuck/fuzzy project, planning anything (a meeting, a launch, a trip), "I have a project but don't know where to start," vertical thinking.

## The premise
*"The most experienced planner in the world is your brain."* Your mind already plans naturally — most planning should be informal **"back-of-the-envelope planning"**, and the goal is simply to *"get projects and situations off your mind, but not lose any potentially useful ideas."*

## The Natural Planning Model — five phases (in order)
1. **Purpose & Principles** — the *why*. Asking "why?" *defines success, creates decision criteria, aligns resources, motivates, clarifies focus, and expands options.* **Principles** = the boundaries you operate within; surface them with: *"I would give others totally free rein to do this as long as they…"* (what?). *"Simple, clear purpose and principles give rise to complex and intelligent behavior"* (Dee Hock).
2. **Outcome Visioning** — the *what* / *wild success*. *"You must have a clear picture in your mind of what success would look, sound, and feel like."* This engages the brain's **reticular activating system** — its filtering "search function," programmed by what you focus on (focus on red and you see red everywhere; *"you won't see how to do it until you see yourself doing it"*). Steps: **(a) view it from beyond the completion date; (b) envision WILD SUCCESS (suspend "yeah, but…"); (c) capture the features/qualities you imagine in place.**
3. **Brainstorming** — the *how* ideas. Once you know the what/why, the mind starts filling the gap. Capture externally (**mind-mapping**, coined by Tony Buzan; clustering; lists). **Keys: don't judge/criticize; go for quantity not quality; keep analysis & organization in the background.** *"The primary criterion must be expansion, not contraction."*
4. **Organizing** — once the ideas are out, structure *emerges*: **identify the significant pieces; sort by components, sequences, and/or priorities; detail to the required degree.**
5. **Next Actions** — *"What's the next action?"* on every front that can move now. Allen: managing next actions per project is *"90 percent of what is generally thought of as project planning"* — the **runway-level** of planning. A project is sufficiently planned when a next action is decided on every component that isn't blocked by another.

## The contrast: reactive / "unnatural" planning
What most people call "planning" is artificial (forced outlines — *"Outlines were easy, as long as you wrote the report first"*) and they resist it. Under stress they run the natural model **in reverse**: panic **Action!** → *"We need to get organized!"* → *"Let's brainstorm!"* → finally someone asks *"what are we really trying to do here?"* (purpose). *"The reactive style is the reverse of the natural model… It's not a matter of whether the natural planning will be done — just when, and at what cost."* Don't open a planning session with "who's got a good idea?" — *"'What's a good idea?' is a good question, but only when you're about 80 percent of the way through your thinking."*

## How much to plan (don't over-plan)
*"As much as you need to get the project off your mind."* Rough distribution:
- **~80%** of projects need only a clear **outcome + next action** (done in your head).
- **~15%** need some external brainstorming (a mind-map, a few notes).
- **~5%** need a deliberate pass through one or more of the five phases.
*"If the project is still on your mind, there's more planning to do."*

## Need more clarity? Move *up* the model
If a project is fuzzy/stuck, shift your thinking up the scale: **next action → organize → brainstorm → vision → purpose.** Re-engaging the *why* and the *wild-success picture* reopens the reticular filter and the "how" reappears.

## From Ch 10 — getting projects under control
- *"Virtually all of us could be doing more planning, more informally and more often."* The biggest gain is capturing the **proactive, creative thinking we don't yet do** — not fancy tools.
- **Bottom-up still rules:** *"If you feel out of control with your current actionable commitments, you'll resist focused planning."* Clear the runway first.
- **Only two kinds of project deserve more planning:** (1) those that **still have your attention** after the next action is set, and (2) those where **useful ideas keep showing up.** For type 1, give yourself a next action *now* that starts the planning ("Draft ideas re X," "Organize notes on X," "Set up planning meeting").
- **Vertical-thinking tools** ("form follows function — great tools trigger good thinking"): whiteboards (*"whenever two or more are gathered, someone should start writing"*), a topic **file folder created the moment you have something to put in it**, simple digital outliners over heavy PM software. Then **block 1–3 hours** of uninterrupted time for project thinking — *"you'll be way ahead of most people."*

## How to apply
- For a stuck project, **run the five phases briefly**: surface the *purpose*, paint *wild success*, *brainstorm* freely (no judging), let structure *organize*, then nail the **next physical action**. Often just clarifying outcome + next action unsticks it.
- Match planning depth to the 80/15/5 rule — don't over-engineer an 80% project.
- If still nagging after a next action exists, it's a "type 1" — schedule deliberate vertical thinking.
